# Human English and Reader-Centred Craft — SRS and Product Documentation

Status: operational reference, 2026-08-27

Original synthesis only; no raw book text is stored.

## Source limits and usable lessons

The Bolitho, Mann, and *Natural English Reading & Writing Skills* files are title-only stubs (111–923 bytes). David’s ceramic manual contributes disciplined observation, controlled fields, classification, glossary use, and traceability from method to conclusion. Gupta’s vocabulary list contributes selective word choice and confusable-word awareness. Pinnacle’s exercises highlight agreement, articles, tense, prepositions, pronouns, parallelism, modifiers, punctuation, spelling, and cohesion, with caution about exam simplification. Betsis and Mamas’s CAE material supports genre, audience, register, task coverage, paragraphing, and recommendations. None of these sources replaces IEEE/ISO requirements engineering or product evidence.

## Documentation standard

Write for the person who must build, test, operate, approve, or use the product. Human technical writing is not casual; it is clear about actors, states, intent, constraints, and consequences. Make interface text warm enough to help and exact enough not to mislead.

### Five passes

1. Name reader, task, state, decision, and required outcome.
2. Choose the correct artefact and structure: requirement, user story, API description, runbook, manual, FAQ, or microcopy.
3. Trace facts, rules, fields, standards, and examples to project evidence; mark context gaps.
4. Revise for unambiguous grammar, controlled terminology, active actors, parallel lists, and varied but restrained sentence rhythm.
5. Proof identifiers, units, examples, links, punctuation, accessibility text, and test oracles; read user-facing text aloud.

## Craft rules

- British English is the default; preserve approved product, local, legal, and technical terms.
- Use a common word where it is exact. Use specialist vocabulary only when defined, necessary, and consistent.
- Check agreement, articles, tense, pronouns, relative clauses, prepositions, parallelism, modifiers, punctuation, spelling, and sentence boundaries.
- Write requirements with an explicit actor, trigger, behaviour, condition, and verifiable outcome. Replace subjective adjectives with measurable thresholds.
- Give explanatory paragraphs one centre of gravity. Do not force a sentence count or use transitions as decoration.
- For design rationale, privately test **decision → evidence → trade-off → failure case → implication**.
- Do not make text “human” by inserting typos, vagueness, unexplained jokes, or fake empathy.

## Microcopy test

Every user-facing message should answer, as space allows: what happened; whether the user’s data or action was saved; what they can do now; and where to get help. Error copy must not blame the user or expose secrets. Labels name the control; help text explains the decision; confirmation text sets a truthful expectation.

## Anti-slop questions

Could this requirement or explanation belong to any product? Add the real actor, field, state, threshold, dependency, or failure case—or cut it. Is every “fast”, “intuitive”, “reliable”, or “seamless” claim measurable? Are terms used consistently? Does a real engineer or user know what to do next? Do not confuse a polished template with a usable specification.

## Evidence record

Record artefact type, reader/task, source and traceability status, terminology checks, test-oracle review, user-facing language review, proof status, unresolved context gaps, reviewer, and date.
